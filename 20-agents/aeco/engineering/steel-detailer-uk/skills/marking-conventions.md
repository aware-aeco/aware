# Marking conventions (UK)

Every fabricated item carries a **unique identifier**, and marking underpins **traceability** back to the mill test certificate.

- **Why:** "Steel from the mills needs to be marked to ensure identification and traceability of the material"; and during erection "a clear and simple marking system for the fabricated components is vital to ensure that all components are correctly located and orientated."
- **Shop (temporary):** components and sub-assemblies are usually identified with **white paint or indelible marker pen** — "regarded as temporary" (they deteriorate outdoors).
- **Permanent:** "a permanent mark should be made to **each welded assembly or separate component** before it leaves the fabrication shop." Within a welded assembly the constituent components are usually traced via **cutting sheets and material inventories** rather than each being permanently marked. The normal permanent method is **hard stamping** (standard, low-stress radiused, or dot-matrix).
- **Fatigue caution:** the sharp "standard hard stamp" is a stress-concentration risk — "identification hard stamp markings should be located in the area of lowest cyclic stress." (Network Rail prohibits the sharp die stamp, accepting only low-stress / dot-matrix.)
- A dot-matrix plate stamp can encode mill, cast number, steel grade and plate number, relating to the test certificate.

## Source

- **BCSA/SCI Guidance Note 5.05 "Marking of steelwork"** (Rev 3, 2015; within SCI P185) — free PDF on steelconstruction.info; corroborated by steelconstruction.info "Fabrication". *Note:* GN 5.05 is bridge-context (its fatigue/hard-stamp emphasis is sharper than typical building work); the marking principles generalise, but building-specific marking clauses sit in the **NSSS (paid)**.
